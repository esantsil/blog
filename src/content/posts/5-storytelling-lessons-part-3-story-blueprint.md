## The Book's Big Promise

Every story, from a fairy tale to a sales email, runs on the same structure. Learn it once and you can reuse it.

McSill defines storytelling as intentional communication that uses story structure to move the reader.

## The Filler You Can Skip

- **Long excerpts of famous openings.** Nice to read, but they don't add to the framework.
- **Fill-in-the-blank worksheets** that eat pages. Do them on your own paper if you want to.
- **Repeated hero's-journey explanations.** The idea comes back several times. You need it once.

## The Golden Takeaway

### The "what happens next?" test

A statement isn't a story. Compare:

- "The old women are on the beach." (So what?)
- "The old women are on the beach, unsure what to do with a dead sailor." (Now I need to know.)

The rule: **you have a story when the audience asks "what happens next?"**

### Two levels: journey and plot

- **Journey:** the order in which things happen.
- **Plot:** the conflict underneath. You want one thing, someone else wants another.

No conflict, no story.

### The 8-step journey, simplified

McSill compresses the classic hero's journey into eight beats:

1. Character lives in their comfort zone.
2. Something is lost, and they want it back.
3. They enter an unfamiliar situation.
4. They adapt.
5. They fight for what they want.
6. They face trials that feel like life or death.
7. They win and return to the familiar world.
8. Life is changed by what they learned.

### Two kinds of hero

- **Action hero** (think James Bond): beats the obstacles, stays the same.
- **Character-driven hero:** ends up changed by a lesson. McSill says this is the model for emotional narratives, from novels to ads to self-help.

### The 5-point premise

Editors use this to judge a manuscript quickly. Answer in order:

1. What's the central situation?
2. Who's the main character?
3. What does that character want by the end?
4. Who or what opposes them?
5. What does the opposition do to stop them?

Question 3 ends as a yes-or-no question that questions 4 and 5 feed. If you can't answer all five, McSill says the manuscript already has a problem.

## Skip the Book, Do This

Answer the five premise questions for something you're working on, in one sentence each.

If one answer is mushy, that's the scene or section to fix first.
