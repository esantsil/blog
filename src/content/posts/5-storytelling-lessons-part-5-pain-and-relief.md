## The Book's Big Promise

Change one element of a story and you change how it lands. This lesson shows you which levers to pull and how to build scenes that keep readers moving.

It also hands you a defense. Once you know the levers, you notice when someone else is pulling them on you.

## The Golden Takeaway

### The five elements of drama

Every story needs:

1. What the main character wants.
2. Who or what blocks them.
3. The disaster that happens when they push too hard.
4. How the character changes.
5. How the _reader_ changes by watching.

### Dissecting a spam email

McSill takes a real marketing email and annotates it. The tricks, in plain words:

- Uses your name, so it feels personal.
- Frames the offer as a gift.
- Makes _you_ the character in the story.
- Says "free," so there's no cost to say yes.
- Limits your options.
- Ends with a command so you act before you think.

He admits he uses some of these techniques himself. His position: knowing them is better than being fooled by them.

### The two-word hype test

When a pitch sounds vague and exciting, ask: **"how so?"**

Run it on phrases like "exclusive secrets revealed" or "proven methodology." If nothing concrete comes back, there's no story.

### The action and reaction loop

Scenes come in pairs:

- **Action scene:** the character wants something, meets an obstacle, gets an outcome.
- **Reaction scene:** the character reflects, faces a dilemma, picks a path.

The decision becomes the hook for the next action scene. Every chapter needs at least one action scene. The reaction scene is optional.

Setting should do two jobs: give the reader hope and a fear that it could get worse.

## Skip the Book, Do This

Open your spam folder. Pick one email and annotate it: which of the six tricks above does it use?

Then ask "how so?" of its biggest claim. Writing something persuasive of your own? Do the same to your draft.
