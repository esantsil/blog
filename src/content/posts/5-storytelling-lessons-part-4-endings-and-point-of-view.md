## The Book's Big Promise

Endings can be learned, not just inspired. McSill gives three methods for landing one and a way to plan the whole story backwards.

He also takes a detour into narrative point of view, which he says can make or ruin your book.

## The Golden Takeaway

### Plan from the end

"End" means two things: where the drama finishes, and what you want the reader to _do_ afterward (buy, keep reading, finish your talk).

His order of work:

1. Decide the ending.
2. Decide what happens to the character.
3. Decide the setting, physical and emotional.
4. Know your character.
5. Give them the tools to win.
6. Choose the opening that leads to that ending.

### Pick a stopping point

Choose the moment after which nothing major happens. Then name your main conflict: person vs nature, person vs person, or person vs self. That tells you what the ending has to resolve.

### Three ways to land it

| Method               | How it works                                                                                                                             |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Explain**          | Recap the character's journey, say why the events mattered, answer "what's this to the reader?" Or speak directly in first person.       |
| **Show**             | Use action and sensory images instead of explaining. Metaphors, open endings or a repeated motif all work here.                          |
| **Follow the logic** | Ask "what happens next?" and "why in this order?" The ending must fit what came before. Add some surprise, so it isn't just predictable. |

One of his examples: in the Odyssey, the monsters matter less than what the hero learns about himself.

### Point of view in one list

- **Omniscient:** the narrator knows everything.
- **Observer:** the narrator only reports what happens.
- **First person:** the narrator is a character, either the protagonist or a witness.
- **Stream of consciousness:** the reader sits inside the narrator's thoughts.

His warning: jumping between characters' viewpoints works for a short story built around a punchline. In a novel, it tends to distance readers and tire them out.

## Skip the Book, Do This

Write your ending in two sentences, then write the first sentence that leads there.

For bonus points, rewrite one paragraph of your own work from a different point of view. It's a fast way to hear what's working.
