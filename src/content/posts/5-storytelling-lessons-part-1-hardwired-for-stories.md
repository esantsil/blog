## The Book's Big Promise

McSill's pitch: you don't need talent handed down from the writing gods. You're born a storyteller.

Our ancestors who told the best campfire stories gained status in the tribe. Wooden spears were optional.

His claim is that anyone writing a novel, a self-help book, or marketing copy can learn the structure underneath. Lesson 1 is the "why".

## The Filler You Can Skip

- **The prehistoric opening.** Charming. Zero actionable content.
- **Four warm-up texts.** You're asked to read them and journal your reactions. Skim them once, or skip them.
- **Ten reflection questions** before the next lesson, including a league table of books read per country. Fun fact, useless for your draft.
- **The author's bio, in third person.** You can Google him.

Skipping all of that costs you nothing.

## The Golden Takeaway

### Stories are about reaction, not approval

The whole lesson boils down to this: a story works when the audience *reacts*.

Love it or hate it, fine. The only real failure is shrugging.

### The "emotional gap" recipe

McSill's recipe for a story that grips, in six steps:

1. Open an emotional gap with a moral dilemma and a conflict.
2. Let the reader crave the missing information.
3. Feed that information in pieces, and make each piece open a bigger gap.
4. Repeat until the reader would pay anything for the answer.
5. Pretend to reveal everything, then open the biggest gap yet.
6. Reveal the missing pieces, give the reader a brief moment to react, and end.

Here's how that maps onto the reader's experience:

| Stage | What you do | What the reader feels |
|---|---|---|
| Open | Plant a dilemma | "Wait, what?" |
| Middle | Drip answers, add complications | "Tell me more." |
| Peak | Fake the final reveal | "I can't stand this." |
| Close | Deliver the missing pieces | "Worth it." |

The reader should leave a little bruised and very satisfied, and ideally wanting more.

> **Bookless translation:** open a question, answer it slowly, and keep asking a bigger one.

## Skip the Book, Do This

Grab the last thing you wrote: an email, a post, a pitch.

Underline the first sentence that makes a reader think "wait, what?" If there isn't one, rewrite your opening so it plants an unanswered question.
